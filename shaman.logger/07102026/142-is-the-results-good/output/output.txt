**Yes, for this hackathon the results are good.** They're statistically solid and they tell a clear story. Some parts are more trustworthy than others, though.

## Strong parts ✅
1. **Different methods agree.** The rank tests, logistic regression and decision tree all pick out the same top factors:
   - juniors: storytelling and maths-stats
   - seniors: openness and conscientiousness

   When three different methods agree, the finding isn't a quirk of one technique.
2. **Clearly better than chance.** The models score AUC 0.90 (JDS) and 0.96 (SDS), against 0.50 for always guessing the larger class. A permutation test gives p = 0.002, so the patterns aren't luck.
3. **Large effects, not just significant ones.** Effect sizes of 0.55–0.77 and Cohen's d of 1.2–1.8 count as large by any standard. With 139–161 people, an effect has to be large to show up this clearly.
4. **Careful method.** Holm correction, bootstrap confidence intervals, repeated cross-validation, shallow models, and a methodology error caught and fixed (the log-loss change). Judges reward this in the "Data Analysis" (30) and "Q&A" (25) sections.
5. **Easy to act on.** "Teach storytelling and statistics; for seniors, openness and conscientiousness matter" turns straight into RQ4 and the implications section.

## Read with caution ⚠️
1. **The data looks too clean to be real-world.** In SDS, *all 52* seniors with openness ≤ 38.5 are low-success, and real HR data almost never splits that perfectly. The brief itself calls it "sample data", so it's probably generated or curated. Say so openly in the report; judges will respect it more than overclaiming.
2. **Small samples mean wide ranges.** JDS AUC varies from 0.77 to 0.99 across folds, so quote the direction and rough size of effects, not exact figures.
3. **Associations, not causes.** Strong storytellers may get hikes *because* they're also visible to managers, for example.
4. **JDS ceiling effect.** Most high performers score the maximum 5, so the scale can't separate the best juniors.
5. **Odds ratios of about 9–10 for SDS are partly a result of the data being so cleanly separated.** Lead with effect sizes and the decision rules, and treat the odds ratios as supporting detail.

## The weakest part is the jobs-data pipeline, not RQ2/RQ3
- Only **57.7%** of recruiter tags map to a skill automatically, and **31% still need human review**.
- Matching job descriptions by meaning **didn't work**, because the descriptions are cut off at about 100 characters. Dropping it was the right call, but it limits RQ1.
- The salary-by-skill results are coarse, because salary comes in bands rather than exact figures.
